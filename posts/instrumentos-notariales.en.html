Janium Collect describes deeds, powers of attorney, and other notarial documents in ISAD-G, and adds to the archival description the data specific to the instrument: its number, volume, folio, and date, the acts it contains, who appears and in what capacity, which properties and amounts are mentioned, and what the public registry shows about its registration. A language model reads the document and proposes those values; then a set of rules that does not go through the model builds the protocol hierarchy, cross-checks what the document says in different places, and rates each record. The schema works for the protocol of a civil-law (Latin) notary’s office in Mexico, in Spain, and in other civil-law notarial countries, with the configuration of each one: the hierarchy adapts to how the protocol is organized in each country, and identification documents and registries are read the way each country uses them.

What is extracted from an instrument

The ISAD-G description of each document carries, in addition to the usual elements, a block with the instrument’s data:

  • The instrument: number, volume, folio, and date, read separately from the cover and from the body so they can be compared.
  • The acts, of which there can be several in a single deed: a sale together with a mortgage cancellation, for example.
  • The appearing parties, with the role each plays in each act, their personal details, and their identification: each country’s identity document and tax ID, such as the CURP, the RFC, and the voter ID card (credencial para votar) in Mexico, or the DNI and the NIF in Spain, and the tax identifier of a foreign party.
  • The power of attorney, when there is one, with its powers and legal basis, and the authority under which the person representing a company acts.
  • The properties, with their ownership history, and the amounts and taxes the instrument declares.
  • Anti-money laundering data, with its notices, and the public registry entry: the registration receipt (boleta) in Mexico or the registration note (nota de inscripción) in Spain.

Each appearing party also becomes an access point in the catalog: a natural person with the name inverted, surname first, and a legal entity as a corporate body. This way, searching the catalog for a person or a company brings together the deeds in which they appear, with the same authority control as the rest of the names.

The record reaches the Janium catalog as a flat file (FLAT), with the instrument’s data in its own tags, so the catalog can search by instrument number, registry folio number, cadastral number, appearing party, or notary.

The protocol hierarchy

In a notarial archive, the deed is kept in a volume, the volume within a notary’s protocol, and the protocol within the notary office. Collect builds that hierarchy and the reference code of each document with rules, from the data it has read, without asking the model for them. In countries where the protocol is not organized in volumes, the hierarchy omits that level, and when the notary has no number, as happens in Spain, the notary is identified by a key derived from the notary’s name.

The hard part is the protocol. Among associated notaries, a deed can be executed before one notary and entered in the other’s protocol, and the deed’s wording is what says so. Collect reads it: if it says “in my protocol,” it assigns the deed to the acting notary, and if it says “in the protocol of Notary number…,” with the number in digits or in words, it assigns it to the notary named. When the wording is not enough, it looks for other deeds in the same volume whose protocol is clear, and if that does not settle it either, the record is left without a reference code and flagged for a person to resolve, with the deed’s wording in view.

What is cross-checked

A deed repeats data in several places, and Collect compares them. The cover and the body have to match on number, volume, folio, and date; the instrument number in the public registry entry has to match the one in the body; the commercial registry folio cited for that authority has to match the one in the entry, and each appearing party who is a natural person has to have some form of identification. A single mismatch is enough to flag the record.

In a 2004 power of attorney, the cross-check flagged that the folio on the cover and the one in the body did not match. When reviewing the document, a person saw the cause: a stamp covered part of the folio on one of the two pages, and the model had read what remained visible. The cross-check does not explain why the numbers differ, but it points to the exact value that needs review.

In Mexican deeds, the CURP, the RFC, and the voter ID card are also checked for format: the CURP and the RFC must have the corresponding structure, and the voter ID card must have one of the valid lengths. That check detects a misread or incomplete ID before it reaches the catalog.

The rating of each record

Each record receives a quality rating that adds the usual ISAD-G rules to seven rules specific to the instrument: that its basic data is present, that the cover matches the body, that the protocol is resolved, that there are appearing parties, that each one is identified, that the public registry entry is consistent, and that the model has left no reading doubts. The public registry rule adds to the rating when the entry is present and does not subtract when it is missing; the other six are required.

Each notary office decides how much each rule weighs. An office that does not register its deeds in a public registry can lower the weight of that rule, and another that works mostly with powers of attorney can raise the weight of identification. The Janium team sets the weights with the notary office when configuring the service.

Limits

  • Substitute and attached (adscrito) notaries. The protocol rules resolve the association between notaries; a deed by a substitute or attached notary is flagged for review.
  • Identification. The format check applies to the Mexican CURP, RFC, and voter ID card, without verifying the check digit; in other countries the identification is extracted as it appears.
  • Mortgages, company incorporations, and notarial certificates (actas). The schema and the rules already cover those acts and the roles each one requires, and they are refined with each notary office’s first documents.
